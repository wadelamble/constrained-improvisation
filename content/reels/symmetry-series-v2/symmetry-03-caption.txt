Symmetry to Waves · 3/25
D₃ is more general than triangles

Any number of objects possess D₃ symmetry. We tend to think of a ‘triangle’s symmetry,’ not the symmetry of two rotations and flips about three axes, but this latter way of thinking in terms of symmetry groups is more general. The group describes the actions and how they compose, rather than the particular outline on which we first noticed them.

The triangle, three circles, three petals, and three rounded bars in the animation have different outlines. Yet a 120° rotation returns each outline to itself, and so does a reflection along an appropriate axis. Each object realizes the same six actions. The rotation cycles three positions, and the reflection exchanges two while leaving the third in place.

This is the definition of symmetry used in physics, where we ask not ‘what symmetry does this or that object have?’ but ‘what mathematical objects realize the actions of a symmetry group?’ The change in emphasis matters. Once we identify the group, we can study its structure without choosing one particular shape as the only object it can act on.

For example, three successive 120° rotations return us to the identity whether the object is a triangle or three petals. The details of the drawing do not enter that rule. Nor do they enter the rule that repeating the same reflection restores the starting arrangement.

We would like to write these actions in a form that can apply to more than a picture. Our next step is to let three positions carry labels or numerical values and describe how the symmetry moves them. The same group will then act on a vector, giving us a mathematical representation of the symmetry.
