# Introduction

"If that's all there is, then let's keep dancing."

—Peggy Lee

"We live in a 4-dimensional space that is curved by gravity." "My time is different from yours." "Objects are in a state of being here and there at the same time — until we observe them." Such expressions strain language and seem beyond the reach of any but those who belong to some monastic order of theoretical physicists, cut off from ordinary language, who we trust, but leave alone. At the same time, terms like "energy" inhabit our metaphors, while their meanings arise from the same artifice as these strange expressions. Behind both lies the unfolding magic of the most ordinary thing — making logical arguments using precise definitions. With faith in this method, we can proceed together along a mystically motivated journey of demystifying modern physics.

This animated book is, hopefully, only as rigorous as it needs to be to act as a faithful companion on this journey. It has two complementary goals. The first is to present the conceptual foundations of modern physics in as logically minimal a way as possible, and the second is to make individual steps along the way accessible. To aid in logical economy, there is no adherence to the history of physics, nor is there an attempt to solve problems or work out details of real-world systems, except by way of illustration. To aid in accessibility, the presentation leans on intuition, visualization, and equations as conceptual schematics. The book is not practically useful enough to be a textbook, but is too heavy with mathematical rigor to be popular science. It belongs to the genre of explainers, animators, and autodidacts. We will explore gravitation and electromagnetism through the theories of relativity and quantum mechanics. While the ideas we discuss include most of the elements used in the Standard Model of particle physics, the exposition stops short of that account.

Our method of argument will be to posit certain principles and see where the repercussions of those lead. We will inevitably leave some principles unstated, and future evidence may rule out some we do state. Indeed, loose ends at the edge of modern physics already lead thinkers to question some of these principles.

- Symmetry. There are symmetries under which physical laws remain unchanged, some of which are self-evident and others which are either implied or posited. For example, if we move a complete system from one location to another, the physical behavior of the system is unchanged.
- Equivalence. If two situations are experimentally indistinguishable, we seek a theoretical account that reveals them as expressions of the same physics. For example, accelerating upward in an elevator in empty space is locally indistinguishable from being held to the ground by gravity. Our theory should therefore express the local equivalence of gravity and acceleration.
- Locality. One event cannot influence another instantly across a distance. Influence must be passed from one point to its neighboring points. We describe this local transmission using fields, physical quantities defined throughout space.
- Memorylessness. The next state of a physical system is influenced only by its current state. That is, the history that led up to a current state is "forgotten" and has no additional influence on the next state.
- Information preservation. Information can be absorbed into the environment, but when a system is considered in its totality, information cannot be lost. This requires that two initially distinct states remain distinct over time. Otherwise, the later state would no longer contain the information to identify the earlier state that produced it. In physical systems, this condition is met by reversibility. That is, the equations that determine a system’s future state equally determine its past state.
- Stationarity. The history of a physical system is one for which the action, a specified weighted sum constructed from the system’s instantaneous state over time, is unchanged to first order under infinitesimal changes to the history with fixed endpoints. For a free particle, this gives straight-line motion. The geometric intuition is that small changes to a straight line segment with fixed endpoints leave its length unchanged to first order.
- Quantum mechanics. This is really the negation of an implicit principle that held sway before quantum mechanics was invented. We might call that principle definiteness. An object has a definite position and definite velocity irrespective of our observations. Quantum mechanics predicts probability distributions for measurement outcomes, and what carries those distributions forward in time cannot, without giving up some other principle, be construed as simply unrevealed definite positions and velocities.

In addition to these principles, there are two more anchors to our reasoning to mention.

- People guess at theories. All the principles above constrain guesses as to what theory explains our observation, but they do not suffice to derive them. For that, we need humans to make and test reasonable guesses. For example, theorists may guess what symmetries are in play or what goes into the weighting that specifies the action.
- Our theories take the form of equations describing a system's evolution in time in which certain constants with physical dimensions appear. These include $G$, the gravitation constant, which maps mass to spacetime curvature, $c$, the speed of light, which limits how fast any influence can travel, and $h$, Planck's constant, which sets the scale at which classical definiteness gives way to quantum probability. The *values* of these constants are not knowable by theory and are obtained solely through experiment.

A note on math. Basic familiarity with calculus and linear algebra (matrices) is assumed. Otherwise, needed math is developed as we go. But we don't shy away from saying things mathematically. It is the most economical and schematic way to summarize conceptual mappings, and it keeps us honest when words invite vagueness that undermines logical argument. Rarely are there more than one or two lines of manipulation to get to a result, and most steps are mirrored in animations. Breezing past all the math is probably inadvisable, but occasionally thinking "I know what that equation is saying, I don't need to unpack it symbol by symbol" is probably fine.

The book begins with a discussion of the symmetry of physical laws. This chapter will introduce concepts from the study of symmetry that are indispensable to the rest of the book. It will also introduce complex numbers, eigenvectors, and Fourier analysis. Additionally, it will draw on the principles of memorylessness and information preservation to make an argument for the abstract form of the equations of evolution that govern wave mechanics, which are one step away from those of quantum mechanics. The second chapter will use the symmetry of physical laws to arrive at the structure of spacetime put forth in the Theory of Special Relativity and discuss this theory in detail. The third chapter will cover the action principle and argue that the method it supplies for finding laws of evolution ties those laws to geometric structures. Together, the spacetime and action chapters will develop the geometric and dynamical meanings of mass, energy, and momentum. The fourth chapter will apply the action principle and the idea of dynamical fields to explore gravitation, described by the Theory of General Relativity, and gauge fields, which form the basis of electromagnetism. Chapter five is about quantum mechanics. It will walk through the formative experiments that required an alternative to classical mechanics, then provide an outline of the theory that leans heavily on the concepts introduced in chapter one on symmetry and introduces the ideas of states living in a superposition of possible outcomes and being nonlocally entangled. This account will focus on the states and evolution of a fixed number of particles, without yet providing a fully local, relativistic field description. The final chapter will develop that description through quantum field theory, treating particles as quantized excitations of fields and arriving at the equations of quantum electrodynamics.

There are two thoughts I would like to share before we get down to business. First, the project of doing physics seems to lead to ever-increasing humility, from Galileo through relativity to quantum mechanics. Our impulse to author theories about what “is,” as if we were an omniscient observer, is replaced by carefully framing theories around what we can observe. We might say that when we reach quantum mechanics, physics stops trying to explain what we presume to be real and contents itself with explaining what information we can acquire. Second, to the extent that quantum theory posits anything that is “there between measurements,” it is described in the mathematics of waves. Music uniquely allows us to perceive meaning in the structure of waves, and the book that follows is, as much as anything, a meditation on the parallels of music and matter.
